BC → RL Transfer
Imitation-to-Reinforcement Learning
Ryan Wang*, Elissa Matlock* Carnegie Mellon University 16831 Final Project Proposal

Random Baselines: Agents acting with random policies across the four MuJoCo environments — Ant, Humanoid Stand, Pusher, and Swimmer. These serve as lower-bound reference points for all method comparisons.

Abstract.
This paper proposes to study warm-start methods in reinforcement learning as a generalizable learning paradigm rather than solely as a tool to accelerate convergence. By initializing policies with demonstrations, behavior cloning–based warm-up stabilizes early learning dynamics and constrains optimization to behaviorally plausible regions of the policy space. We evaluate two initialization strategies — standard behavior cloning with RL fine-tuning (PostBC) and an integrated imitation+RL approach (IMRL) — across four continuous-control MuJoCo tasks from Gymnasium, comparing against a pure on-policy baseline (REINFORCE+GAE) and an off-policy state-of-the-art (TD3). To assess transferability, we further examine how warm-started policies adapt under embodiment changes and compare their sample efficiency during downstream fine-tuning.

Learning Curves.
Mean return (±1 std, 3 seeds) over 500k environment steps. Dotted lines mark the expert and random baselines for each environment.
HalfCheetah-v4. Expert 9 417 · Random −284. TD3 is the only method to escape the random baseline, climbing steadily to ~4 000 by 500k steps. BC, PostBC, and IMRL remain anchored near zero — the expert gait requires high-frequency coordination that imitation alone cannot capture.
Hopper-v4. Expert 1 228 · Random 19. All methods show meaningful learning. TD3 achieves the highest final return (~1 500, above expert) but with high variance. REINFORCE+GAE and IMRL converge more smoothly to ~600–800. BC provides a head-start before plateauing early.
Walker2d-v4. Expert 3 765 · Random 3. TD3 achieves peak returns near 2 500 but is highly variable with periodic collapses. IMRL follows a more stable trajectory, reaching ~1 200 reliably. Pure BC and PostBC plateau quickly without the RL signal to escape the imitation regime.
Humanoid-v4. Expert 434 · Random 123. The flagship result: IMRL reaches expert-level performance (~430) by 300k steps. TD3 plateaus below random and never recovers — the 17-DoF exploration space is intractable for pure RL. The imitation signal is essential here.
Analysis.
HalfCheetahTD3 wins A planar cheetah rewarded for forward velocity. The dense, smooth reward landscape is precisely where off-policy RL excels. Imitation helps little here: the expert gait requires subtle body-pitch dynamics that BC cannot capture from limited demonstrations, leaving IMRL pinned near zero alongside BC.
HopperTD3 wins (noisy) A single-legged hopper with delicate balance. TD3 exceeds expert return but at the cost of high variance — it discovers aggressive policies absent from the demonstrations. IMRL learns stably and safely, with the imitation signal acting as a regularizer that prevents early policy collapse.
Walker2dTD3 wins (high var.) A bipedal walker requiring coordinated gait. TD3 peaks highest but collapses periodically. IMRL's joint optimization maintains stability while still improving, making it the most reliable mid-range choice when variance tolerance is low.
HumanoidIMRL wins! A 17-DoF full humanoid — the hardest task. Pure RL (TD3) fails completely; the exploration problem is intractable without guidance. IMRL's integrated training leverages the imitation signal throughout, reaching expert-level performance. In high-dimensional control, combining imitation with RL is not just helpful — it is essential.
Summary. Across these four environments a consistent pattern emerges: for tasks with simple, dense reward landscapes off-policy RL (TD3) remains the strongest stand-alone baseline. As task complexity grows — particularly in the full 17-DoF Humanoid — the exploration bottleneck becomes prohibitive for pure RL, and IMRL's combination of imitation with reinforcement learning is the only method to match expert behavior. PostBC offers a lightweight middle ground but does not match the sample efficiency of joint optimization.
